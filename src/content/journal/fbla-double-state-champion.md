## One result, two different kinds of thinking

The two events tested different parts of how I approach business.

Business Ethics required me to analyze an organizational failure, identify the incentives and decisions behind it, and recommend a response that could stand up ethically and operationally. Retail Management demanded broad command of day-to-day retail strategy through an objective test.

Winning both mattered because the preparation could not be reduced to a single strength. One event rewarded structured analysis and persuasive recommendations; the other required disciplined study across a wide field of retail knowledge.

## Business Ethics: from greenwashing to accountable strategy

My Business Ethics case centered on Geo Motors and its “Geo Pulse,” a vehicle marketed as zero-emissions and exceptionally eco-friendly even though its broader lifecycle involved carbon-intensive manufacturing, risky mineral sourcing, and energy-heavy artificial-intelligence features.

I diagnosed the problem as more than inaccurate advertising. Marketing had focused on tailpipe emissions while ignoring the environmental and human consequences elsewhere in the value chain. The deeper failure was a disconnect between marketing, engineering, supply-chain realities, and executive accountability.

My proposed response worked at three levels:

1. **Consumer transparency:** replace broad environmental slogans with lifecycle disclosures, public methodology, and clear communication about tradeoffs.
2. **Governance:** establish an independent ethics and ESG review board with authority over sustainability claims and cross-functional approval from engineering, sustainability, legal, and ethics leaders.
3. **Product integrity:** reduce impact through battery chemistry choices, lower-compute software modes, traceable sourcing, recycling, and restoration in affected communities.

The central idea was simple: an ethical response could not stop at better public relations. It had to change what consumers were told, how leaders were held accountable, and how the product was built.

> Together, the recommendations addressed transparency at the consumer level, accountability at the organizational level, and integrity at the product level.

## Retail Management: breadth, speed, and judgment

Retail Management was a different challenge. FBLA describes the high-school event as a **50-minute, 100-question objective test** covering areas such as foundational retail knowledge, selling, pricing, market planning, inventory control, visual merchandising, display techniques, and product mix.

Preparing meant learning how individual decisions connect across an entire retail operation: how merchandising influences customer behavior, how inventory affects cash and service, how pricing supports positioning, and how store operations shape the customer experience.

## What the first year taught me

Winning twice did not make the work feel finished. It made the next level feel real.

The experience showed me that I could move between ethical strategy and operational detail, then communicate each in the form the event demanded. More importantly, it gave me a standard for the work that followed: understand the system, identify the real decision, and build an answer that works beyond the presentation room.
